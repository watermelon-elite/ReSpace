# ReSpace: a launch film about the life inside the room

**Current production treatment:** see the agreed [three-minute landscape script and storyboard](../respace-launch-script-storyboard.md). It leads with “This dorm room could belong to anyone,” uses an imagined furnished future and reserves short supporting sections. This page preserves the earlier 65-second research proposal.

**Working recommendation: “You've changed. Has your room?”** Begin with recognizable human behavior, let the room reveal a mismatch, and introduce ReSpace as the way to explore a better arrangement. The film should feel like one story from opening image to product proof to human payoff.

This is an original creative proposal derived from the audiovisual research, not a claim that a reference film proves psychological effects or guarantees views. The evidence links below distinguish observations from proposed ReSpace scenes. The full research index records viewing coverage.

## The larger idea

Spatial personality becomes interesting when it describes a person rather than a decorating category. An inherited chair, a guitar that cannot be reached, a desk facing a wall, and the space someone clears when a friend visits tell different stories. A room can contain someone's tastes and still fit their life poorly. That tension is more useful for this launch than a lecture about how environments affect people.

The opening should show a life changing while the room remains familiar. One person now works at home, wants friends over, or is returning to a hobby. Pick a single clear need that the working ReSpace build can help address. The audience recognizes the situation; the software arrives when that person needs to make a decision.

## A proposed 65-second spine

These are **proposed production timings for a new ReSpace film**, not timestamps from the references. They are starting points for an animatic, not a measured formula for retention.

| Time | Picture and action | Sound / narrative job |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 s | A distinctive locked wide. Someone tries a familiar action and the arrangement gets in the way: an exercise mat cannot open, a chair blocks a route, or a hobby is buried behind an old work setup. Choose one. | Start with the physical action and its recognizable sound. Optional original line: “You've changed. Has your room?” |
| 3–10 s | Two short returns to the same room show the person's present life and the arrangement's outdated assumptions. A personal object remains the visual anchor. | Build a rhythm from room sounds and concise voiceover. The humor or surprise should be visible with sound off. |
| 10–16 s | The person pauses and looks at the room. A doorway, wall edge or chair movement motivates a match into actual scan footage. | Briefly simplify the mix. Bridge line, if needed: “What would this room look like if it started with you?” |
| 16–25 s | The real ReSpace name and capture UI enter. Show the same room being scanned and its recognizable geometry. Keep a human hand or face in the sequence. | A clear musical change introduces possibility. Explain the action in ordinary language, without architecture diagrams or model-provider names. |
| 25–38 s | Show one meaningful goal or constraint in the implemented interface, then the corresponding proposal. The viewer should understand what was requested and what changed. | Give the input enough time to read. A restrained action sound connects it to the result. If processing is compressed, use an honest editorial transition. |
| 38–48 s | Inspect the proposed arrangement from a useful angle. If demonstrated in the build, show one real Product decision or one language edit that reinforces the opening need. | Let the strongest proof breathe. Remove additional features that require a new explanation. |
| 48–59 s | Return to the original viewpoint and activity. Use either a clearly identified proposal or a separately staged physical after-scene. The same personal object belongs in the new composition. | Let the person and room supply the emotional payoff. Avoid a narrator claiming the app changed their mental health. |
| 59–65 s | Clean ReSpace identity and one actual destination. | One next step appropriate to availability: join the waitlist, request access or download. Choose the truthful one before final export. |

## Three openings worth testing

1. **Identity mismatch:** the same room, three small indications of a life that has changed. Opening thought: “You've changed. Has your room?” This is the best fit for the requested broader picture.
2. **The blocked activity:** start with an immediately readable physical obstacle, then reveal the person's intention. This gives the film an accessible problem even without voiceover.
3. **One room, different lives:** several people occupy the same composition in successive shots, each using it differently. The question becomes what this particular person needs. This needs more casting and careful continuity, so it is a secondary production option.

These variants should share the same proof section. That makes comparison useful: the hook changes while the product explanation remains stable. No variant has an established performance advantage from this research.

## Production choices that create polish

**Design the room before designing the edit.** Choose a space with an obvious layout, depth and one memorable object. Establish two or three repeatable camera positions. Stable geography allows fast changes without forcing the audience to reorient.

**Use different tempos for different jobs.** The opening can change rapidly; the unfamiliar product operation needs more time. A held result after a burst of activity is often more persuasive than another quick cut. Motion within a shot can create energy without increasing cut frequency.

**Make sound carry the transition.** Record the actual chair scrape, key turn, fabric movement or footstep that matters to the opening action. Let a related sound or gesture bridge into the scan. Build toward the result, then leave room for the human payoff. Track choice and exact instrumentation remain creative decisions; copying a reference soundtrack is unnecessary.

**Make the 3D output intentional.** Give the actual app scene a clean presentation with consistent room orientation. Typography and framing can make a non-photoreal proposal feel designed. Do not disguise an external render as the app's generated output.

**Complete one understandable task.** Scan → intent → inspectable proposal is the central proof. A real Product or supported edit may add credibility if it improves that story. A list of every feature weakens the film's focus.

**Choose the working result before locking the opening.** Capture a real, convincing proposal first. Identify the specific room need it answers, then build the opening around that need. This keeps the film's promise and evidence connected and prevents an ambitious hook from demanding a capability the app cannot demonstrate.

**Design the mobile framing deliberately.** Room-wide geography and readable phone UI need different compositions. Plan both before filming, keep important action away from platform overlays, and adapt the inserts for the intended aspect ratio. A landscape desktop capture shrunk into a vertical frame will rarely explain a spatial decision clearly. This is a production recommendation, not a measured finding from the reference campaigns.

## Product and evidence boundaries

The consolidated [SPEC.md](../../../SPEC.md) governs planned behavior: LiDAR-capable iPhone capture, personal-room design, real verified Products and merchant offers, language edits, and versioned results. It excludes furniture dragging/direct rotation and requires real shopping rather than placeholder cards. This research did not validate the running implementation. Every filmed operation must come from a demonstrated build or be clearly identified as a concept.

A physical room makeover, animated geometry and a proposed 3D design are different things. Editorial effects may connect them, but should not imply live AR erasure, instantaneous delivery or automatic physical movement. Current processing time must not be inferred from an edited commercial. The final CTA must match real availability.

## How to judge the first cut

Show an animatic to someone unfamiliar with ReSpace. Ask what the opening person wanted, what the app did, and which image they remember. If the answers are vague, fix the visual chain before increasing edit speed. Then compare alternate openings with the same body and CTA. This is a proposed evaluation method; the reference films do not provide retention curves or evidence that their editing caused their view counts.
